SHAM-Bench: A Span-level Hallucination Benchmark Across
Multiple Tasks
Sicong Huang and Ian Lane
University of California, Santa Cruz
shaun213@ucsc.edu,ialane@ucsc.edu
Abstract
Span-level hallucination detection pinpoints un-
supported content in LLM outputs, enabling ac-
tionable feedback and targeted mitigation. Ex-
isting evaluations for span-level hallucination
detection methods are fragmented across tasks,
annotation formats, and metrics, and most span-
level datasets cover only a single NLG domain,
making it difficult to assess whether a detection
method actually generalizes across tasks and
domains. In this paper, we introduceSHAM-
Bench(Span-levelHallucination benchmark
AcrossMultiple tasks), a unified benchmark for
span-level hallucination detection spanning six
NLG tasks, aggregated from existing human-
annotated datasets totaling 6,461 examples un-
der a common schema and evaluation protocol.
Using SHAM-Bench, we evaluate existing hal-
lucination detection methods and find that fine-
tuned detectors often perform well in-domain
but degrade under distribuiton shift, whereas
prompt-based methdos with reasoning are more
robust across tasks. Our analysis further shows
that subtle unsupported content remains diffi-
cult to detect and span-level evaluation is still
challenged by ambiguous span boundaries and
noisy labels. We release the dataset, evaluation
code, and a public leaderboard to support future
research on broadly generalizable hallucination
detection.
1 Introduction
While Large Language Models (LLMs) are ex-
tremely effective at generating fluent text, they of-
ten produce hallucinations, generating content that
isfactually incorrect with respect to world knowl-
edge or is unsupported by the available evidence
(Huang et al., 2025a; Zhang et al., 2025). Detecting
hallucinations in LLM outputs is important for ap-
plications ranging from dialog assistants (Li et al.,
2023) and decision-support systems (Singhal et al.,
2023; Brynjolfsson et al., 2023) to complex agen-
tic workflows (Lin et al., 2025). Early work in
hallucination detection focused on coarse-grained,
response-level decisions (Wang et al., 2020; Fab-
bri et al., 2021; Adlakha et al., 2024; Healy et al.,
2026). While useful for screening, response-level
detection does not identifywherehallucination er-
rors occur, limiting its value for actionable feed-
back.
Span-level hallucination detection addresses this
limitation by localizing the specific words or
phrases in a generation that are unsupported or
factually incorrect (Liu et al., 2022). Rather than
rejecting an entire response, a span-level detector
can preserve supported content while identifying
hallucinated segments. This is especially valuable
for post-editing workflows (Tonmoy et al., 2024)
and for providing fine-grained training signals for
hallucination mitigation (Wen et al., 2025).
Despite its practical importance, span-level hal-
lucination detection remains difficult to evaluate
systematically. Most annotated resources provide
only sentence- or response-level labels (Lin et al.,
2022), too coarse to test whether methods identify
precise unsupported spans. Datasets with span-
level annotations remain scarce and typically focus
on a single task (Tang et al., 2023; Vazquez et al.,
2025), limiting how far their conclusions gener-
alize across domains. Moreover, differences in
annotation formats and evaluation metrics further
complicate direct comparisons of detectors.
To address these challenges, we introduce
SHAM-Bench, theSpan-levelHallucination
benchmarkAcrossMultiple tasks. SHAM-Bench
is a unified benchmark for span-level hallucination
detection. It consolidates nine task partitions ag-
gregated from six human-annotated datasets into
a single standardized multitask benchmark. The
benchmark spans six NLG tasks: open-domain
QA, context-based QA, data-to-text generation, bio-
graphical generation, summarization, and machine
translation, totaling 6,461 test set examples. By
normalizing annotation formats and using standard
arXiv:2610.27370v1  [cs.CL]  19 Oct 2026
evaluation metrics across all datasets and tasks,
SHAM-Bench allows a meaningful comparison of
hallucination detection methods across a wide vari-
ety of tasks.
Using SHAM-Bench, we perform a comprehen-
sive evaluation of seven existing hallucination de-
tection methods and analyze why they succeed or
fail across tasks. Our results show that fine-tuned
detectors often perform well in narrow domains
but struggle under distribution shift, while prompt-
based methods with reasoning are more robust
across domains. Furthermore, our analysis shows
that both annotation and modeling challenges ex-
ist. Span annotations can suffer from ambiguous
span boundaries and noisy labels, and subtle unsup-
ported content remains difficult to detect reliably
even for the most capable model.
Taken together, our findings suggest that advanc-
ing span-level hallucination detection requires both
accurrate and reliable annotations across tasks to
support evaluation and more sensitive detection
methods to identify subtle errors. We hope SHAM-
Bench will serve as a valuable resource for the
community, allowing practitioners to understand
the pros and cons of different hallucination de-
tection methods, and to advance the state-of-the-
art in span-level hallucination detection. Further,
we hope that future work would build on SHAM-
Bench to incorporate more tasks and datasets cap-
turing the fuller spectrum of NLG tasks to support
more generalizable hallucination detection.
2 Related Works
2.1 Detecting Hallucinations in LLMs
Most early approaches to detect hallucinations in
LLM outputs aim to provide response level judge-
ments. Starting with a reference and a response,
BERTScore (Zhang* et al., 2020) judges a response
by scoring the embedding similarity between a ref-
erence and the response while BARTScore (Yuan
et al., 2021) uses the log-likelihood of the response
directly as the score. Taking a reference-free ap-
proach, SelfCheckGPT (Manakul et al., 2023) es-
timates factuality by sampling multiple responses
and measuring their mutual inconsistency.
Later approaches aim to provide more fine-
grained hallucination judgements. For instance,
AlignScore (Zha et al., 2023) and MiniCheck (Tang
et al., 2024) fine-tune models to determine whether
a sentence aligns or is supported by a given context.
RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) and FA V A (Mishra
et al., 2024) use span-annotated data to fine-tune
models to achieve span-level hallucination detec-
tion. QAGS (Wang et al., 2020) and FactScore
(Min et al., 2023) operate at the fact level by ei-
ther asking and answering factual questions about
the response or by decomposing the response into
individual facts and verifying each one.
Another class of methods leverage model-
internal signals. LLM-Check (Sriramanan et al.,
2024) uses internal attention maps, hidden activa-
tions, and output probabilities to detect hallucina-
tions within a single response, while Ogasa and
Arase (2025) exploits attention irregularities across
informative layers to localize hallucinated spans.
Leveraging uncertainty, Farquhar et al. (2024) in-
troduces semantic entropy as a hallucination signal,
showing hallucinated claims exhibit higher entropy.
2.2 Evaluating and Benchmarking
Hallucination Detection Methods
Most prior benchmarks for hallucination detection
are limited in one of two ways: they either provide
only coarse-grained annotations at the response or
sentence level, or they provide fine-grained span
annotations but only for a single task.
TruthfulQA (Lin et al., 2022) evaluate response-
level hallucinations on QA task and find that larger
models are more prone to misconceptions. Ag-
greFact (Tang et al., 2023) aggregates nine summa-
rization datasets for response-level evaluation, find-
ing that no single metric performs best across the
individual datasets and HADES (Liu et al., 2022)
gathers token-level annotations for free-form gen-
eration, showing that surrounding context length
greatly affects how well hallucinations can be de-
tected. While these approaches offer valuable in-
sight when evaluating hallucination detection on
these tasks, the evaluations in each work are limited
to a single task.
HaluEval (Li et al., 2023), though spans mul-
tiple tasks, only perform hallucination detection
at the response level. The same is true for LLM-
AggreFact (Tang et al., 2024) which aggregates 10
datasets for sentence-level factual consistency eval-
uation. Mu-SHROOM (Vazquez et al., 2025) and
PsiloQA (Rykov et al., 2025), despite providing
span-level annotations for multiple languages, fo-
cus exclusively on the QA task. To our knowledge,
RAGTruth (Niu et al., 2024) and Zhou et al. (2021)
are the only two benchmark with human labeled
span-label annotations of hallucinations that span
multiple tasks.
3 SHAM-Bench
SHAM-Bench, theSpan-levelHallucination
BenchmarkAcrossMultiple Tasks is a reference-
based benchmark for span-level hallucination de-
tection in LLM outputs. It combines all currently
available human-annotated span-labeled hallucina-
tion detection datasets, covering nine task-specific
test sets from six datasets, into a single unified
multitask benchmark. SHAM-Bench spans six
NLG tasks: open-domain QA, context-based QA,
data-to-text, biographical generation, summariza-
tion, and machine translation. The responses in the
benchmark are generated by modern LLMs, allow-
ing SHAM-Bench to reflect the real-world condi-
tions and LLM-generated outputs which hallucina-
tion detection systems are likely to encounter. Ad-
ditionally, the summarization and QA tasks include
multiple dataset partitions, enabling SHAM-Bench
to identify generalization failures across datasets
that single-dataset benchmarks cannot expose.
3.1 Benchmark Datasets
FaithBench(Bao et al., 2025) is a summarization
faithfulness benchmark containing 750 summaries
(75 passages× 10 LLMs), where annotators la-
bel spans asquestionable,benign, orunwanted.
We convert these into binary hallucinated/non-
hallucinated span labels.FA V ABench(Mishra
et al., 2024) contains∼1k model responses to open-
domain queries with human annotations that lo-
calize factual errors at the span level with a fine-
grained error-type taxonomy. After processing, we
retain 266 examples, each augmented with GPT-
5-retrieved context for use as reference evidence.
HalluEntity(Yeh et al., 2025) is an entity-level
hallucination dataset built from GPT-3.5-generated
biographies. Following the FActScore pipeline
(Min et al., 2023), atomic facts are verified against
Wikipedia and mapped to entity mentions, yielding
157 manually verified examples. Each is paired
with GPT-5-retrieved context.Mu-SHROOM
(Vazquez et al., 2025) provides human-annotated
span-level hallucination labels for LLM-generated
answers to open-domain questions across 14 lan-
guages. We use the English subset of 150 exam-
ples, each augmented with GPT-5-retrieved con-
text.RAGTruth(Niu et al., 2024) is a span-
annotated RAG benchmark covering question an-
swering, data-to-text, and summarization. Each
of the 2,700 test examples pairs a user query and
evidence context with an LLM-generated response.

3.2 Dataset Schema, Tasks, and Statistics
Standardized schema.To reconcile differences
in dataset formats, we convert every instance to a
unified schema with four main fields, enabling con-
sistent preprocessing and evaluation across tasks:
• question: the original user query or instruc-
tion that elicited the response.
• context: auxiliary input required to answer
the query (e.g., evidence passage, structured
record, source document, or target-language
text). For tasks that do not provide additional
input (e.g. open_qa, open_biography), this
field contains retrieved context that answers
the question, so that the hallucination detec-
tion methods that require context can use it.
• response: the model-generated response to be
evaluated for hallucinations.
• hallu_spans: a list of human-annotated hallu-
cinated spans in the response text.
Tasks.We assign each instance from the six
source datasets (§3.1) to one of six tasks that cover
common NLG use cases:
• open_qa: open-domain QA where the answer-
ing model is given only a question, without
any supporting evidence or context.
• context_qa: QA conditioned on an explicit
context passage; the model receives both a
question and a provided context and is ex-
pected to answer using that context.
• data-to-text: data-grounded generation
where the query targets information in struc-
tured data (e.g., a JSON record) and the model
produces a natural-language output.
• open_biography: biographical generation
where the model is prompted to write a bi-
ography of a real person.
• summarization: summarization of an input
document; the model is given a source text
and asked to produce a concise summary.
• machine_translation: translation of a source
text from one language to another.
We provide a small validation set by sampling
five instances for each (dataset, task)-pair, yielding
45 instances in total. This validation set is used to
explore the effectiveness of prompt optimization
explored in section §4 of this paper.
SHAM-Bench contains 6,461 examples span-
ning six tasks sourced from six datasets (Table 1).
Summarization is the largest category, consisting of
56% of the total examples, while open_biography
is comparatively small, being only 2.4% of the to-
tal. Response lengths differ markedly by task; ma-
chine_translation averages 27.5 words in response
while open_qa averages 192 words. Hallucination
rate is likewise highly non-uniform across tasks
and datasets. At the task level, average hallucina-
tion rates range from∼0.04 in data2txt and con-
text_qa to 0.227 in open_biography and 0.294 in
summarization. The dataset-level breakdown fur-
ther reveals within-task differences; summarization
contains hallucinated content in 3% of token on
RAGTruth but 46.6% on Zhou, and open_qa hallu-
cinates 14.2% of tokens on FA V ABench vs. 32.6%
on Mushroom. Differences in annotation guide-
lines and the model used to generate responses
likely contribute to the variation in hallucination
rates across tasks and datasets.
3.3 Metrics and Evaluation
We use F1 score as the primary evaluation metric.
This choice follows prior span-level hallucination
detection work including Mishra et al. (2024); Niu
et al. (2024); Zhou et al. (2021). Moreover, since
SHAM-Bench standardizes detector outputs into
binary token-level labels, hallucination detection
can be naturally evaluated as a binary classification
problem. F 1 score balances precision and recall
and is therefore not dominated by the majority non-
hallucinated class. Appendix B provides further
discussion of alternative metrics
SHAM-Bench supports evaluation at two lev-
els of granularity: token-level and sentence-level.

a model-generated response r, and human-labeled
hallucinated spans S⊂r . A detector predicts ei-
ther hallucinated spans ˆS or sentence-level labels ˆz.

4 Hallucination Detection Methods
Using SHAM-Bench, we evaluate seven existing
high-performing hallucination detection methods
in three major categories, namely discriminatively
trained, generatively trained, and prompt-based
methods. Appendix A provides detailed method
descriptions.
Discriminatively trained methods. MiniCheck
(Tang et al., 2024), which is trained on synthetic
data to judge whether claims are supported, and
AlignScore(Zha et al., 2023), which is trained
on multiple datasets to score alignment, both de-
tect hallucination at the sentence level.PsiloQA
(Rykov et al., 2025), trained on synthetic data, and
LettuceDetect(Ádám Kovács and Recski, 2025),
trained on human-annotated data, instead detect
hallucination at the token level. These methods
fundamentally treat hallucination detection as a bi-
nary classification problem whether at the sentence
or token level.
Generatively trained methods. FA V A(Mishra
et al., 2024) andRAGTruth(Niu et al., 2024) are
train using the language modeling objective with
FA V A using synthetic data and RAGTruth using
human annotated data. They both identify hal-
lucinated content by generating the unsupported
content as text spans.
Prompt-based methods.Prompt-based detec-
tors use an LLM as a direct hallucination span
extractor by providing the relevant information and
instruction to the LLM as prompt. We evaluate both
hand-crafted and automatically optimized prompts
(Huang et al., 2025b). Prompt templates are pro-
vided in Appendix F.
5 Benchmarking Results
5.1 Prompt-based methods are robust across
different tasks
Results in Table 2 indicate prompted GPT-5-mini
(with reasoning, no prompt optimization) is the
most balanced method across all tasks. At span-
level F1, the prompted method achieves an overall
score of 0.498, placing second only to RAGTruth
(0.518). It ranks first on three of the six tasks:
open_qa (0.402), open_bio (0.525), and mt (0.435),
while falling behind RAGTruth or LettuceDetect
on the three tasks that those models were directly
trained on, which is further discussed in §5.2. At
sentence level, it achieves the highest overall score
(0.691), surpassing all discriminatively and gen-
eratively fine-tuned methods. It outperforms all
discriminatively trained methods on four tasks,
falling slightly behind MiniCheck on summariza-
tion and LettuceDetect on context_qa. The prompt-
based method also shows the most consistent per-
formance across tasks, with the lowest standard
deviation in task-level F1 scores (0.07), suggesting
that prompting is less prone to large task-specific
performance swings.
5.2 Finetuned methods can perform well in
narrow domains but struggle to generalize
PsiloQA: strong where hallucinations are fre-
quent, but over-predicts elsewhere.PsiloQA
obtains its best span-F1 on Mushroom QA (0.556)
and Zhou summarization (0.547), two datasets with
high hallucination rates, but it performs poorly on
context_qa (0.072) and data2txt (0.089), where hal-
lucinations are rare. The same pattern holds for
sentence-level F 1: PsiloQA achieves its highest
score on Zhou summarization, where 91% of re-
sponses are labeled as containing hallucinations.
However, this over-prediction leads it to classify
nearly all examples as hallucinated, dragging down
its overall performance.
RAGTruth and LettuceDetect: dominant in-
domain, weak elsewhere.Although RAGTruth
achieves the highest overall span-F1 (0.518), this
aggregate masks a sharp split between its training
tasks and everything else. Table 3 makes this split
explicit: RAGTruth scores 0.835 on the RAGTruth
summarization partition and 0.865 on RAGTruth
QA at the span level, but is only mediocre on other
datasets. The same pattern appears at sentence
level, where it leads on its own partitions yet ranks
among the worst on summarization Zhou and QA
Mushroom. LettuceDetect, fine-tuned on the same
data, behaves similarly: strong on RAGTruth parti-
tions but weak elsewhere, except on Zhou summa-
rization, where over-flagging is less costly because
most responses contain annotated hallucinations.

6 Challenges in Hallucination Detection
To better understand the system behavior observed
in §5, we examine two complementary sources
of difficulty. First, using RAGTruth and PsiloQA
as representative cases, we analyze how the dis-
tribution of fine-tuning data shapes detector pre-
dictions. Second, we analyze failure cases from
prompt-based methods and identify several factors
that continue to limit span-level hallucination de-
tection.
6.1 Fine-tuned models tend to follow their
training data distribution.
As shown in Figure 1, hallucination rates in the
RAGTruth summarization training set are concen-
trated near the low end. The model fine-tuned on
this data closely follows the training distribution,
regardless of the distribution of the evaluation set.
This behavior of output distribution aligns well
with the RAGTruth test set, but not with Faith-
Bench, whose distribution is less skewed, or Zhou
et al., which has an additional mode at the high end.
As a result, the RAGTruth-trained model places
disproportionate weight on low hallucination rates
and tends to under-predict hallucinations. The Psil-
oQA model exhibits the opposite bias. Although
hallucination rates in the PsiloQA training set are
more evenly distributed, 96% of its training exam-
ples contain hallucinations. Consequently, when
evaluated on QA datasets whose hallucination rates
are concentrated near the low end, the model tends
to over-predict hallucinations, resulting in system-
atically inflated hallucination estimates.
Fundamentally, these results reflect a train-test
distribution shift, in which the training distribution
does not match the evaluation setting. For prac-
titioners, this suggests that class balancing alone
is insufficient. Instead, training data should be
curated to ensure adequate representation of hal-
lucination cases, especially rare error types, while
preserving or explicitly modeling the expected de-
ployment distribution.

6.2 Prompt-based methods exhibit mixed
failure modes
To characterize prompt-based failure modes, we
divide ground-truth spans into two groups: those
that overlap with at least one prediction and those
with no overlapping prediction. The first group
captures cases in which the detector identifies the
relevant factual issue, even if its predicted bound-
ary differs from the human annotation. The second
captures hallucinations that are missed entirely. For
these missed spans, we use GPT-5.4 to assign one
of five error categories:labeling error,overgener-
alization,unsupported detail,misattribution, and
subjective language; category definitions appear in
Appendix C.

covers 7,542 of 10,066 ground-truth spans (74.9%)
with at least some overlap. This suggests that many
apparent span-level errors arise from boundary dis-
agreement rather than complete failure to recognize
the underlying hallucination. Among overlapping

7 Conclusion
We presented SHAM-Bench, a unified benchmark
for span-level hallucination detection across di-
verse generation tasks. Our evaluation shows that
fine-tuned detectors often perform well in-domain
but degrade sharply outside their training distribu-
tions, whereas prompt-based methods with reason-
ing are more robust and consistent across tasks.
At the same time, ambiguous span boundaries,
noisy labels, and subtle unsupported content re-
main open challenges for reliable span-level eval-
uation. SHAM-Bench provides a foundation for
future research on accurate, broadly generalizable
hallucination detection.
8 Limitations
Task coverage.SHAM-Bench covers a diverse
set of NLG tasks, but it does not exhaustively
represent all useful language generation settings.
Many important tasks are still missing because the
benchmark is built from currently available human-
annotated span-level hallucination resources. Our
goal is therefore not to provide a complete taxon-
omy of NLG tasks, but to establish a first extensible
multi-task benchmark for span-level hallucination
detection. As more human-annotated span-level
datasets become available, additional tasks and do-
mains can be incorporated into SHAM-Bench.
RAGTruth over-representation.RAGTruth
contributes 2,675 examples to SHAM-Bench
across context QA, data-to-text, and part of the
summarization task, accounting for approximately
41% of the benchmark. This over representation
provides an advantage to detectors fine-tuned on
the RAGTruth training set, meaning that strong
overall performance does not necessarily reflect
broad generalization. As additional tasks and
datasets are contributed to SHAM-Bench in the
future this imbalance will diminish.
Legacy model outputs.A substantial portion of
the Zhou data was generated by pre-LLM-era mod-
els. The resulting errors differ significantly from
hallucinations produced by modern LLMs. This
limits the extent to which performance on this sub-
set reflects detector behavior in contemporary set-
tings.
Annotation inconsistency.The source datasets
used in SHAM-Bench were annotated by different
groups following different annotation guidelines,
which leads to substantial variation in how hal-
lucination spans are defined and marked. Some
datasets favor minimal spans, while others use
broader spans. In aggregate, however, we believe
that the rank of the hallucination detection meth-
ods will not be affected by variation in annotation
styles across the individual the datasets.
Annotation quality.In this work, we did not per-
form quality control of the annotations provided
in the source datasets. The quality of annotations
across the datasets are known to vary widely, which
limits the extent to which the benchmark can faith-
fully measure a model’s ability to identify halluci-
nations. However, again in aggregate, we believe
that the relative ranking of hallucination detection
methods is unlikely to be affected by the annotation
quality of individual datasets.
Automated failure classification.The error cate-
gories used in our prompt-based failure analysis are
assigned by GPT-5.4 rather than manually verified
by expert annotators. As a result, the category pro-
portions should be interpreted as an approximate
diagnostic analysis rather than as definitive class
labels.
Coverage of evaluated methods.In this work,
we did not evaluate methods that require knowledge
of or internal access to the generating model, such
as hidden-state-based detectors or self-consistency
methods. To ensure faithful implementation and ac-
curate evaluation, we encourage researchers devel-
oping such techniques to evaluate them on SHAM-
Bench and submit their results to the SHAM-Bench
Leaderboard.
Potential risks.Although SHAM-Bench is in-
tended as a research benchmark, it may still intro-
duce downstream risks if used incautiously. Im-
perfect hallucination detectors can be over-trusted
in deployment; false negatives may allow unsup-
ported content to pass as reliable, while false pos-
itives may incorrectly flag factual content and re-
duce the usefulness of model outputs. In addi-
tion, some benchmark instances involve generation
about real people (e.g., the open_biography task),
so detector outputs may affect how biographical
or factual claims are interpreted in downstream ap-
plications. We therefore view SHAM-Bench as
a research evaluation resource and not as a suffi-
cient basis for high-stakes real-world deployment
decisions.

A Hallucination Detection Method Details
Discriminatively trained methods. MiniCheck
(Tang et al., 2024) frames hallucination detection
asevidence-grounded claim verification: given a
generated sentence and supporting context, it pre-
dicts whether the sentence is supported. The mod-
els are trained with two synthetic data pipelines:
Claim-to-Doc (C2D), which decomposes claims
into atomic facts and builds multi-sentence evi-
dence, and Doc-to-Claim (D2C), which derives
summaries as claims from real documents and con-
structs unsupported pairs by removing sentences
from the original document. This yields sentence-
level fact-checking models.
AlignScore(Zha et al., 2023) frames hallucina-
tion detection as aninformation alignmentproblem:
for a text pair (a, b), a unified alignment function
f: (a, b)→y estimates whether all information
in b is present in, and non-contradictory with, a.
The model is trained with multi-task learning on
15 datasets spanning seven NLP task families, and
training is further augmented with synthetic posi-
tives from back translation and synthetic negatives
from masking/infilling. At inference time, it splits
the output into sentence-level claims and chunks
evidence into segments, computes the alignment
score of each claim against all evidence chunks,
and aggregates these scores into an overall score;
sentences with low alignment are treated as likely
hallucinated.
PsiloQA(Rykov et al., 2025) is an en-
coder token-level hallucination detector based on
ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024). It takes in
context–question–response triples and predicts a
binary classification label for each response token.
The model is trained on the English subset of the
PsiloQA (Rykov et al., 2025) training data, which is
synthetically generated from a three-stage pipeline:
creating question-answer pairs from Wikipedia,
eliciting potentially hallucinated answers from di-
verse LLMs in a no-context setting, and automati-
cally annotating hallucinated spans by comparing
responses to the golden answers and retrieved con-
text using GPT-4o.
LettuceDetect(Ádám Kovács and Recski, 2025)
is a token-level hallucination detection model built
on ModernBERT (Warner et al., 2024). Similar
to PsiloQA, it processes concatenated context–
question–response triples and predicts whether
each response token is hallucinated. In con-
trast to PsiloQA, LettuceDetect is trained using
RAGTruth’s dataset, which is labeled by human
annotators.
Generatively trained methods. FA V A(Mishra
et al., 2024) frames hallucination detection as a
generation problem. Given an LLM response and
retrieved evidence, the model generates the re-
sponse with markers that wrap unsupported content.
FA V A is trained on synthetic data generated by first
creating factually grounded seed passages from
Wikipedia and then injecting errors while mark-
ing corrupted spans. An LLM is trained on the
resulting input–output pairs (erroneous input + evi-
dence→ marked output). Hallucinated spans are
identified by marking them as unsupported content
during generation.
RAGTruth(Niu et al., 2024) adopts a simpler
generative approach by fine-tuning an LLM to di-
rectly output hallucinated text spans when given a
context–response pair. The LLM is fine-tuned on a
large-scale training set (∼18,000 examples) includ-
ing context_qa, data2text, and summarization tasks,
which consists of human-annotated span-level la-
bels of hallucinated content.
Prompt-based methods.We also evaluate
prompt-based detection methods that treat an LLM
as a direct hallucination span extractor. Following
Huang et al. (2025b), given a task question, evi-
dence context, and generated response, the prompt
asks the LLM to compare the response against
the evidence and output hallucinated text as ex-
plicit spans, i.e. substrings of the response. This
approach requires no additional model training
while still producing span-level outputs suitable for
SHAM-Bench evaluation. Beyond a hand-crafted
instruction prompt, we apply prompt optimization
(Huang et al., 2025b) using MiPROv2 (Opsahl-Ong
et al., 2024) to maximizeF 1 scores.
B Metric Choice
F1 score is intentionally simple, but it is well
aligned with the span-detection task evaluated in
SHAM-Bench. Because gold annotations and
model predictions are represented as sets of hal-
lucinated response tokens, precision measures how
many predicted hallucinated tokens are correct and
recall measures how many gold hallucinated tokens
are identified. F 1 combines these quantities into
a single positive-class overlap score while avoid-
ing the inflated performance that can arise from
metrics dominated by the much larger set of non-
hallucinated tokens.
Other overlap-based metrics would capture
closely related information. For example, Vazquez
et al. (2025) uses intersection-over-union (IoU);
for a fixed predicted token set and gold token set,
IoU and F1 are monotonic transformations of the
same overlap counts, so they are expected to pro-
duce similar rankings. Threshold-independent met-
rics such as AUPRC and AUROC used in Yeh
et al. (2025) can be useful when systems output
calibrated confidence scores, but several SHAM-
Bench systems, including prompt-based and gen-
erative span-extraction methods, output discrete
spans without comparable scores. Balanced accu-
racy and macro-F1 that provide balanced weighting
of positive and negative classes reported in Bao
et al. (2025) are also less directly aligned with our
goal of measuring detection quality for the positive
hallucinated-token class. We therefore use token-
level F1 as the primary span-level metric and report
sentence-level F1 as a complementary view when
methods operate at sentence granularity.
C Failure Category Definitions
We classify missed ground-truth spans into five
categories for the prompt-based failure analysis in
Section 6. These categories are intended to distin-
guish likely annotation artifacts from substantive
detector failures, and to separate different types of
unsupported content.
Labeling error.This category covers cases in
which the missed “ground-truth hallucination” is
not clearly a detector failure because the gold an-
notation itself is questionable. The annotated span
may be directly supported by the source, may con-
stitute an acceptable paraphrase or modest infer-
ence, or may reflect an annotation-boundary arti-
fact, such as a fragment, duplicate span, discourse
marker, or non-factual token. In such cases, the de-
tector may have appropriately ignored the span or
identified the substantive hallucination with a dif-
ferent boundary. This category therefore captures
annotation-side noise rather than a clear failure to
detect unsupported content.
Unsupported detail.This category covers gen-
uine hallucination misses in which the response in-
troduces concrete information that is not supported
by the source. The unsupported content may be
an added attribute, procedural step, recommenda-
tion, purpose, causal explanation, exact descriptor,
or schema-derived claim. It also includes cases in
which missing or null metadata is converted into an
affirmative factual assertion, such as treating an un-
known ambience field as evidence that a restaurant
is “casual” or treating an unspecified amenity as
absent. The defining property is that the response
adds a specific fact-like detail that is not licensed
by the available evidence.
Overgeneralization.Overgeneralization errors
occur when the response remains loosely connected
to the source but changes the strength, scope, cer-
tainty, or characterization of the supported claim.
The response may turn narrow evidence into a
broader rule, extrapolate from a single example to
a general conclusion, or replace a source-supported
description with a stronger or subtly different one.
Unlike unsupported-detail errors, these cases often
do not introduce a wholly fabricated fact; instead,
they overextend or semantically drift from what the
source establishes.
Misattribution.This category covers cases in
which content may be related to the available evi-
dence but is assigned to the wrong source, entity,
task direction, comparison frame, status, or eviden-
tial basis. For example, a response may claim that
a detail comes from a particular passage when it
does not, merge information from separate cases,
apply facts about one entity to another, or answer
the reverse of the intended task. The central error
is therefore not merely a lack of support, but the
placement of otherwise plausible information in
the wrong evidential or contextual frame.
Subjective language.This category covers un-
supported evaluative, promotional, sentimental, or
ambience-related wording. The response may add
judgments such as “friendly,” “cozy,” “popular,”
“beautiful,” or other claims about reception, service
quality, or atmosphere that are not grounded in the
source. Although these spans may sound plausible
and are often softer than concrete factual fabrica-
tions, they still introduce unsupported characteri-
zations. We separate this category because such
language reflects a distinct form of embellishment
rather than a specific factual addition.
