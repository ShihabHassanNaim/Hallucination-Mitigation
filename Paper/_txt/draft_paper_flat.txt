 1.
 Introduction
 
 Languages
   
 generated
 by
 Large
 Language
 Models
 (LLMs)
 closely
 resemble
 those
 of
 human
 beings
 yet
 they
 still
 have
 a
 tendency
 to
 "hallucinate",
 meaning
 to
 generate
 text
 that,
 although
 grammatically
 correct,
 is
 factually
 wrong
 or
 not
 supported
 at
 all.
 A
 thorough
 overview
 of
 available
 detection
 methods
 points
 to
 the
 distinction
 between
 two
 main
 aspects
 of
 the
 problem:
 factuality
 hallucination,
 when
 the
 generated
 content
 is
 inconsistent
 with
 the
 veriﬁable
 world
 knowledge,
 and
 faithfulness
 hallucination
 when
 output
 drifts
 from
 the
 user
 original
 intention
 the
 contextual
 information
 provided,
 or
 its
 logical
 internal
 consistency,
 [1].
 The
 magnitude
 of
 the
 issue
 is
 quite
 huge:
 an
 analysis
 of
 hallucination
 datasets
 collected
 automatically
 has
 come
 up
 with
 an
 estimate
 that
 open-
 and
 closed-source
 models
 hallucinate
 about
 20%
 to
 30%
 of
 all
 responses.
 Furthermore,
 it
 depends
 on
 the
 topic
 and
 on
 the
 speciﬁc
 model,
 respectively
 [12].
 The
 reason
 why
 hallucinated
 pieces
 of
 text
 are
 hard
 to
 detect
 is
 because
 they
 sound
 natural
 and
 conﬁdent,
 so
 one
 cannot
 detect
 it
 by
 reading
 alone
 and
 that
 is
 why
 there
 has
 been
 a
 massive
 increase
 of
 research
 work
 in
 the
 area
 of
 hallucination
 detection
 [1].
 
   
 
 Shared
   
 evaluation
 efforts
 have
 guided
 the
 way
 detection
 systems
 have
 been
 developed
 and
 compared.
 The
 SemEval-2024
 Task6
 shared
 task
 (SHROOM)
 set
 a
 common
 benchmark
 in
 identifying
 model-agnostic
 and
 model-aware
 hallucinations
 through
 a
 simple
 yes/no
 (ﬂuent
 overgeneration)
 classiﬁcation.
 
 The
 various
 approaches
 revealed
 by
 participating
 in
 the
 task
 include:
 methods
 that
 measure
 output
 similarities
 by
 matching
 against
 target
 text
 reference,
 using
 pre-trained
 embedding
 models
 like
 LaBSE,
 T5,
 and
 DistilUSE
 [5],
 and
 exploring
 the
 degree
 to
 which
 those
 embeddings
 can
 reveal
 content
 that
 has
 been
 hallucinated
 [6];
 model-agnostic
 methods
 where
 word
 alignment
 plus
 ﬁne-tuned
 BERT-based
 classiﬁers
 are
 the
 main
 features,
 and
 model-aware
 approaches
 where
 attention
 values
 and
 layer
 activations
 from
 the
 LLM's
 internal
 data
 are
 tapped.
 When
 the
 two
 are
 joined
 the
 accuracy
 rate
 hits
 above
 80%
 [7].
 Besides
 classiﬁcation,
 hallucination
 detection
 has
 moved
 into
 generation
 tasks
 as
 well:
 in
 machine
 translation,
 hallucinations
 have
 emerged
 when
 a
 translation
 gets
 rid
 of
 its
 source
 completely
 and
 this
 detachment
 is
 measured
 by
 source
 contribution.
 This
 source
 contribution,
 together
 with
 cross-lingual
 sentence
 similarity
 detection,
 has
 improved
 detection
 accuracy
 a
 lot
 
   [4].
 
 Rather
   
 than
 only
 concentrating
 on
 the
 hallucinations
 that
 appear
 through
 the
 text
 itself,
 some
 research
 has
 focused
 on
 directly
 ﬁnding
 out
 how
 the
 hallucinations
 are
 generated
 from
 the
 model
 itself.
 INSIDE
 has
 shown
 that
 the
 inner
 workings
 of
 LLM
 can
 be
 used
 to
 detect
 hallucinations
 by
 using
 EigenScores,
 a
 method
 that
 captures
 semantic
 diversity
 in
 the
 responses,
 and
 restricting
 too
 high
 activation
 which
 causes
 overconﬁdent
 generations
 from
 the
 model.
 [9].
 The
 LLM-Check
 is
 even
 more
 advanced
 as
 it
 creates
 "closed-model"
 scenarios
 by
 analysing
 hidden-state
 representation
 eigenvectors
 and
 determining
 how
 uncertain
 it
 is
 to
 generate
 each
 token
 of
 output.
 This
 enables
 the
 identiﬁcation
 of
 domain-speciﬁc
 manifestations
 of
 hallucinations
 through
 the
 heterogeneity
 of
 their
 generation.
 [10]
 In
 the
 family
 of
 methods
 that
 ensure
 self-consistency
 among
 answers
 by
 generating
 them
 independently
 and
 checking
 their
 compatibility
 with
 one
 another
 are
 indeed
 quite
 effective,
 but
 they
 are
 very
 time-consuming
 because
 they
 require
 multiple
 runs
 of
 the
 system;
 this
 drawback
 is
 overcome
 by
 the
 Decoding
 Memory
 Pipeline
 which
 makes
 use
 of
 the
 common
 parts
 of
 preﬁx
 tokens
 among
 generations
 and
 results
 in
 speed
 improvement
 up
 to
 threefold
 with
 no
 detection
 performance
 losses.
 
   [11].
 
 
 
 Regarding
   
 the
 data
 aspect
 of
 the
 work,
 AutoHall
 proposes
 a
 data
 preparation
 method,
 which
 is
 model-agnostic
 for
 hallucination.
 They
 use
 fact-check
 resources
 that
 are
 openly
 available
 to
 us.
 Moreover,
 AutoHall
 designs
 a
 self-examining
 detector
 that
 does
 not
 make
 use
 of
 training
 data
 which
 can
 be
 considered
 almost
 an
 autonomous
 blackbox
 as
 it
 works
 based
 on
 checking
 for
 self-contradiction.
 The
 authors
 demonstrate
 the
 usefulness
 of
 their
 new
 strategy
 by
 accuracy
 over
 a
 dataset
 
   [12].
 
 Practically
   
 utilizing
 these
 methods
 is
 where
 applied
 systems
 come
 in.
 A
 case
 in
 point
 is
 a
 real-time
 system
 that
 works
 to
 uncover
 hallucinations
 in
 large
 language
 models
 (LLMs)
 and
 combines
 multiple
 techniques:
 fetching
 evidence
 from
 trusted
 sources,
 checking
 the
 similarity
 of
 the
 meaning
 from
 sentence-to-sentence,
 ensuring
 consistency
 within
 multiple
 responses
 of
 a
 model,
 and
 a
 ﬁnal
 decision
 step
 that
 determines
 if
 a
 result
 is
 factual
 or
 made-up
 [2].
 The
 success
 achieved
 by
 this
 method
 is
 in
 line
 with
 an
 old
 saying:
 "rely
 on
 one
 technique
 you
 miss
 what
 it's
 blind
 to,
 combine
 several
 and
 the
 combined
 approach
 brings
 in
 the
 maximum
 result"
 
   [2]
 
 However,
   
 certiﬁed
 analysis
 of
 these
 latest
 results
 shows
 that
 there
 is
 a
 reason
 to
 be
 cautious
 about
 depending
 exclusively
 on
 methods
 which
 detect
 through
 embedding
 and
 entailment.
 It
 comes
 to
 light
 that
 embedding-
 and
 entailment-based
 detection
 approaches
 work
 quite
 well
 on
 hallucinations
 which
 are
 made
 up
 to
 appear
 like
 real
 samples
 but
 are
 actually
 synthetic,
 however,
 when
 presented
 with
 the
 real
 cases
 of
 hallucinations
 from
 models
 trained
 by
 the
 reinforcement
 learning
 from
 human
 feedback,
 these
 detection
 methods
 can
 no
 further
 detect
 anything.
 The
 hardest
 examples
 of
 these
 real
 hallucinations
 are
 almost
 completely
 semantically
 different
 from
 authentic
 ones.
 This
 kind
 of
 scenario
 has
 been
 called
 a
 Semantic
 Illusion
 [13].
 This
 paper
 actually
 provides
 us
 with
 a
 direct
 reason
 not
 only
 to
 design
 veriﬁcation
 pipelines,
 which
 are
 based
 on
 embedding
 similarity,
 but
 also
 to
 include
 other
 methods
 of
 
   veriﬁcation.
 
 This
   
 research
 proposes
 a
 13-point
 method
 to
 check
 hallucinations
 and
 verify
 facts
 after
 a
 retrieval-augmented
 generation
 (RAG)
 system
 presents
 the
 outputs
 obtained
 via
 an
 aggregation
 of
 strategies.
 Verifying
 through
 this
 method
 is
 not
 restricted
 to
 a
 single
 ﬁnal
 step;
 rather,
 it
 is
 an
 open
 process
 consisting
 of
 multiple
 levels
 with
 some
 levels
 of
 checks.
 At
 ﬁrst,
 the
 user's
 query
 is
 reformulated
 using
 different
 means
 to
 make
 it
 more
 detailed
 through
 enriching
 it
 with
 terms
 by
 zero-shot
 Name
 Entity
 Recognition
 (NER)
 techniques
 like
 GLiNER,
 spaCy
 and
 also
 an
 instruction-ﬁne-tuned
 Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct
 generation
 module
 (Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct
 as
 a
 secondary
 
   option).
 
 Database
   
 of
 retrieved
 documents
 that
 has
 been
 done
 is
 further
 queried
 through
 a
 mix
 of
 dense
 and
 sparse
 retrieval
 methods
 (dense
 BGE-M3+FAISS
 and
 also
 BM25
 sparse),
 that
 are
 merged
 with
 reciprocal
 rank
 fusion,
 followed
 afterwards
 by
 reranking
 cross-encoder
 is(BGE-reranker-v2-m3).
 For
 each
 statement
 of
 the
 model
 response,
 the
 evidence
 veriﬁcation
 is
 performed
 using
 a
 multilingual
 NLI
 model
 (mDeBERTa-v3,
 XNLI),
 which
 is
 exactly
 the
 approach
 of
 the
 NLI
 veriﬁer
 who
 was
 the
 top
 performer
 in
 the
 shared
 tasks
 described
 in
 [8].
 Besides,
 a
 conﬁdence
 score
 estimator
 on
 veriﬁcation
 signals
 (temperature
 scaling
 and
 isotonic
 regression)
 produces
 calibrated
 scores
 based
 on
 uncertainty
 [4],
 [10],
 and
 there
 is
 a
 regeneration
 action
 that
 takes
 over
 the
 rewriting
 of
 the
 claims
 from
 the
 answer
 that
 have
 not
 been
 veriﬁed,
 thereby
 introducing
 the
 notion
 of
 feedback
 from
 the
 detection
 phase
 to
 the
 response
 mitigation
 phase
 
   
 
 
 
 
   The
 approach
 of
 the
 pipeline
 is
 to
 avoid
 relying
 solely
 on
 vector
 similarity
 as
 a
 method
 for
 proving
 correctness
 because
 it
 only
 provides
 a
 factuality
 signal
 at
 best
 and
 is,
 therefore,
 by
 no
 means
 a
 reliable
 one
 [13].
 In
 our
 case,
 the
 pipeline
 relies
 primarily
 on
 facts
 retrieved
 from
 the
 database
 and
 judgment
 based
 on
 Natural
 Language
 Inference
 (NLI)
 decisions
 sentence-by-sentence.
 The
 ﬁnal
 judgment
 along
 with
 the
 level
 of
 certainty,
 the
 verdict
 on
 each
 claim,
 and
 a
 summary
 of
 all
 thirteen
 phases
 of
 the
 process
 are
 provided
 together.
 In
 fact,
 by
 default,
 every
 component
 of
 the
 pipeline
 uses
 real
 HuggingFace
 checkpoints,
 and
 for
 each
 of
 the
 thirteen
 steps
 it
 is
 a
 separate,
 traceable
 module
 that
 also
 records
 its
 own
 evidence
 and
 decisions.
 The
 backend
 for
 exposing
 the
 system
 is
 through
 FastAPI,
 and
 the
 Flask
 front-end
 allows
 users
 to
 look
 at
 everything
 as
 it
 happens.
 Finally,
 the
 system
 was
 evaluated
 on
 HaluEval
 and
 TruthfulQA
 benchmarks
 in
 addition
 to
 using
 generation
 metrics(BERTScore,
 ROUGE-L)
 together
 with
 detection
 metrics.
 
 
 2.
 Related
 Works
 
 2.1
 Taxonomies
 and
 Benchmarks
 Bestetti
   
 et

 [1]
 provide

 of

 detection

 distinguishing
 two
 types
 of
 hallucination:
 factuality
 hallucination
 where
 generated
 text
 contradicts
 what
 is
 a
 common
 world
 knowledge
 and
 faithfulness
 hallucination,
 which
 is
 output
 generated
 text
 being
 in
 contradiction
 with
 input
 or
 with
 the
 context.
 The
 SHROOM
 shared
 task
 [3]
 has
 introduced
 detection
 as
 a
 challenge
 with
 two
 main
 tracks,
 model-agnostic
 and
 modelAware,
 of
 binary
 classiﬁcation
 of
 overgeneration
 errors,
 thereby
 allowing
 research
 community
 to
 have
 a
 uniform
 
   benchmark.
 
 2.2
 Sentence-Similarity
 Detection
 Dale
   
 et
 al.
 have
 developed
 a
 method
 to
 identify
 machine
 translation
 hallucinations
 that
 are
 translations
 not
 closely
 connected
 with
 the
 source,
 cross-lingual
 sentence
 similarity
 helped
 them
 to
 increase
 accuracy.
 Iordanidou
 et
 al.
 investigated
 various
 pre-trained
 encoders
 (LaBSE,
 T5,
 DistilUSE)
 when
 distinguishing
 between
 hallucinations
 and
 factual
 claims,
 while
 Bendahman
 et
 al.
 demonstrate
 how
 these
 embeddings
 highly
 react
 to
 hallucinations.
 Score
 calculation
 through
 embedding
 is
 very
 straightforward
 and
 quite
 eﬃcient
 yet
 the
 results
 rely
 solely
 upon
 the
 encoder,
 that
 means,
 it
 is
 highly
 
   subjective.
 
 2.3
 Model-Aware
 Detection
 The
   
 method
 INSIDE
 [9]
 consists
 of
 eigen-score
 calculation
 over
 sampled
 response
 embeddings
 plus
 feature
 clipping
 in
 order
 to
 control
 overconﬁdence.
 By
 combining
 eigen-analysis
 of
 hidden
 layer
 features
 with
 token-level
 uncertainty,
 the
 system
 LLM-Check
 [10]
 works
 for
 closed
 models.
 Both
 methods
 address
 the
 question
 of
 designing
 the
 model
 either
 transparent
 (white-box)
 or
 opaque
 
   (black-box).
 
 2.4
 Ensemble
 Systems
 Researchers
   
 Arzt
 et
 al.
 combine
 a
 model-agnostic
 (word
 alignment
 using
 machine
 translation
 and
 BERT
 for
 classiﬁer)
 and
 a
 model-aware
 (activations,
 attention)
 detector,
 and
 the
 combined
 detector
 reaches
 accuracies
 of
 80.6%
 and
 81.7%
 on
 the
 two
 SHROOM
 tracks.
 
 
 On
 the
 other
 hand,
 Grigoriadou
 and
 colleagues
 combine
 an
 NLI
 model
 with
 a
 detector
 and
 achieve
 77.8%
 and
 79.9%
 respectively.
 Both
 sets
 of
 authors
 show
 that
 blending
 detectors
 of
 varying
 types
 outperforms
 having
 only
 
   one.
 
 2.5
 Self-Consistency
 and
 Eﬃciency
 Despite
   
 requiring
 repeated
 generation,
 self-consistency
 checking
 is
 a
 highly
 effective
 method.
 In
 their
 work,
 Gao
 et
 al.
 [11]
 take
 advantage
 of
 overlapping
 tokens
 for
 generations
 with
 the
 same
 preﬁx
 in
 order
 to
 shorten
 the
 generation
 duration.
 This
 clever
 method
 even
 resulted
 in
 up
 to
 3x
 faster
 generation
 with
 AUROC
 being
 retained
 to
 the
 same
 level
 as
 
   before.
 
 2.6
 Datasets
 and
 Black-Box
 Detection
 AutoHall[2]
   
 generates
 hallucination
 datasets
 specialized
 for
 each
 model
 from
 fact-checking
 corpora.
 According
 to
 this
 project,
 hallucination
 rates
 are
 approximately
 20-30%
 for
 most
 models.
 Additionally,
 it
 comes
 with
 a
 zero-resource
 self-contradiction
 detector
 which
 performs
 better
 than
 its
 baseline
 competitors.
 
   [2]
 
 2.7
 Limits
 and
 Applied
 Systems
 According
   
 to
 [13],
 Sinha
 and
 his
 collaborators
 demonstrated
 that
 detection
 of
 true
 hallucinations
 from
 RLHF-aligned
 models
 using
 embedding-based
 methods
 is
 totally
 ineffective.
 The
 problem
 is
 that
 the
 hardest
 hallucinations
 look
 very
 close
 semantically
 to
 correct
 answers,
 so
 people
 still
 believe
 in
 that
 semantic
 illusion.
 Keerthi
 et
 al.
 [12]
 built
 a
 Streamlit
 app
 in
 real-time,
 which
 combines
 evidence
 retrieval
 system,
 semantic
 similarity,
 and
 self-consistency
 as
 features.
 They
 assert
 that
 such
 combined
 techniques
 work
 better
 than
 single
 ones,
 however,
 they
 also
 admitted
 that
 some
 problems,
 related
 to
 evidence
 quality,
 speed,
 evaluation,
 and
 benchmarks,
 remain
 
   unresolved.
 
 2.8
 Positioning
 of
 Our
 Work
 The
   
 literature
 points
 to
 the
 fact
 that
 sentence
 similarity
 detection
 is
 cheap
 but
 fragile
 (e.g.,
 [4],
 [5],
 [13]).
 Internal-state
 methods,
 on
 the
 other
 hand,
 are
 powerful
 but
 require
 model
 access
 [9],
 [10].
 Ensembles
 are
 effective
 but
 multiply
 components
 [6],
 [7].
 And
 self-consistency
 increases
 cost
 [11],
 [12].

 Chapter
 3:
 Research
 Methodology
 3.1
 Research
 Design
 The
   
 project
 basically
 adopts
 a
 design-and-evaluation
 approach.
 A
 complete
 hallucination-detection
 and
 fact-veriﬁcation
 pipeline
 for
 retrieval-augmented
 generation
 (RAG)
 systems
 is
 developed,
 implemented,
 and
 evaluated
 from
 start
 to
 ﬁnish.
 The
 pipeline
 consists
 of
 thirteen
 stages
 and
 is
 mapped
 one-to-one
 to
 the
 architecture
 diagram
 of
 the
 project.
 Every
 stage
 is
 implemented
 as
 an
 independently
 testable
 software
 module.
 What
 this
 design
 is
 fundamentally
 based
 on
 is
 the
 idea
 that
 one
 should
 not
 limit
 hallucination
