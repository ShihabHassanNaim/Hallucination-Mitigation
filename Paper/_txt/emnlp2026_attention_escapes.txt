When Attention Escapes: Diagnosing and Mitigating Hallucination in
Retrieval-Augmented Generation

Abstract
Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) mit-
igates hallucinations by grounding genera-
tion in retrieved evidence, yet failures persist
when models underutilize the retrieved context.
While prior work attributes this to conflicts be-
tween external context and internal parametric
knowledge encoded in Feed-Forward Networks
(FFNs), our empirical analyses reveal that FFN-
derived signals are unstable and insufficient
for reliable diagnosis across diverse contexts.
We therefore propose theAttention Escape
Score (AES), a training-free diagnostic frame-
work that provides a mechanistic lens on RAG
hallucinations via dynamic attention routing
failures. We models the failures as weakened
Semantic Anchoring, increasedContextual Dis-
traction, and heightenedGeneration Inertia,
moving beyond static component-level anal-
yses. Extensive experiments show that AES
significantly outperforms state-of-the-art base-
lines with robust cross-model generalization.
Furthermore, we introducePruning Escape
Routes (PER), a lightweight inference-time
intervention that suppresses hallucinations by
attenuating identified escape pathways, achiev-
ing improved faithfulness with negligible degra-
dation to complex reasoning and general ca-
pabilities. The code is available at https:
//github.com/Eason8/AES-RAG.
1 Introduction
Large Language Models (LLMs) (Llama Team,
2024; OpenAI, 2023; Yang et al., 2025) demon-
strate remarkable performance in knowledge-
intensive applications, while their real-world de-
ployment remains fundamentally limited by hal-
lucinations (Huang et al., 2025). Retrieval-
Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as
a widely adopted strategy to mitigate hallucina-
tions by grounding generation in retrieved docu-
ment (Ayala and Béchard, 2024; Gao et al., 2023).

Nevertheless, even with retrieved context, mod-

evidence, leading to unsupported or contradictory
outputs (Niu et al., 2024).
Hallucination detection has shifted from black-
box uncertainty heuristics to white-box diagnos-
tics leveraging internal states. While prior work
trained supervised probes on hidden representa-
tions (Zhang et al., 2025), RAG-oriented methods
increasingly focus oncontext–knowledge interac-
tionsignals (Xu et al., 2024), often quantifying
utilization via contrasting the external context with
parametric contributions from Feed-Forward Net-
works (FFNs) (Sun et al., 2025; Yeh et al., 2025).
However, in RAG settings, FFNs inevitably pro-
cess both attention-routed contextual information
and internal parametric knowledge. This dual role
entangles contextual transformation with memo-
rized knowledge activation, making FFN-derived
signals difficult to interpret reliably. Our empiri-
cal analysis confirms this limitation: FFN-based
indicators exhibit substantial instability across lay-
ers and samples, failing to consistently distinguish
whether the model relies on retrieved evidence or
internal priors. Motivated by this ambiguity, we

instead focus on theattention mechanism(Bast-
ings and Filippova, 2020), which directly governs
information routing during generation. This per-
spective is especially important under thelost-in-
the-middlephenomenon (Liu et al., 2024), where
hallucinations arise when attention fails to reach
relevant evidence in long contexts. Therefore, un-
derstanding attention routing dynamics is crucial
for diagnosing hallucination in RAG systems.
Building on this insight, we propose theAtten-
tion Escape Score (AES), a training-free frame-
work that diagnoses hallucinations as dynamic
routing failures along three dimensions:Seman-
tic Anchoring,Contextual Distraction, andGen-
eration Inertia(Figure 1). Furthermore, we intro-
ducePruning Escape Routes (PER), a lightweight
inference-time intervention that attenuates identi-
fied escape pathways. Crucially, PER suppresses
hallucinations with negligible degradation to com-
plex reasoning and general capabilities. Exten-
sive experiments across diverse LLMs demonstrate
that AES consistently outperforms strong baselines,
while PER yields clear mitigation gains.
In summary, our core contributions are:
• An empirical study revealing that FFN dynam-
ics in RAG systems lack stable, discriminative
patterns for hallucination diagnosis, motivating
a strategic shift toward attention routing.
• TheAttention Escape Score (AES)for
training-free detection via routing failures,
andPruning Escape Routes (PER)for
lightweight, inference-time mitigation that pre-
serves general model capabilities.
• Extensive evaluations demonstrating that AES
robustly outperforms state-of-the-art baselines
across diverse models and datasets, while PER
significantly improves generation faithfulness.
2 Related Work
Hallucination Detection in LLMs.Early hallu-
cination detectors mostly adoptblack-boxheuris-
tics based on output uncertainty (Ren et al., 2023;
Malinin and Gales, 2021; Manakul et al., 2023;
Zhang et al., 2023). More recentwhite-boxmeth-
ods leverage internal states, often via supervised
probes over hidden representations (Zhang et al.,
2025; Orgad et al., 2025; CH-Wang et al., 2024; Li
et al., 2023), but typically require labeled data and
can be brittle under domain or model shifts.
In RAG systems, hallucination detection meth-
ods increasingly focus oncontext–knowledge inter-
actionsignals that reflect how models trade off re-
trieved evidence against parametric knowledge (Xu
et al., 2024). ReDeEP (Sun et al., 2025) provides a
mechanistic analysis of the roles of attention heads
and FFNs, while LUMINA (Yeh et al., 2025) pro-
poses utilization-based metrics; both link hallucina-
tions to reduced context use and a stronger reliance
on internal knowledge.
Attention Heads and Information Routing in
RAG.Mechanistic interpretability studies (Ols-
son et al., 2022; Zheng et al., 2024; Ma et al.,
2025; Jin et al., 2024) show that attention heads in
Transformer-based LLMs exhibit diverse, function-
ally specialized behaviors and contribute unevenly
to information routing. This is particularly salient
in RAG systems, where long and heterogeneous
contexts make evidence access non-trivial. Prior
work (Ma et al., 2025; Cheng et al., 2025) finds
that grounded generations often depend on a subset
of attention heads maintaining focus on retrieved
evidence, whereas hallucinations coincide with de-
graded routing and misdirected attention.
Building on these insights, we directly analyze
dynamic attention routing through head-level pat-
terns. Our method tracks how attention shifts
among retrieved evidence, irrelevant context, and
generated history during decoding, and leverages
these routing dynamics to diagnose hallucination.
3 Preliminary Analysis: The Role of
FFNs in RAG Hallucination
Recent studies hypothesize that RAG hallucina-
tions arise from conflicts between retrieved context
and FFN-hosted parametric knowledge (Geva et al.,
2021; Sun et al., 2025; Yeh et al., 2025). Motivated
by this view, we examine whether FFN dynamics
provide reliable hallucination signals. Specifically,
we analyze whether FFN updates exhibit discrim-
inative patterns between hallucinated and factual
generations using Llama2-7B (Touvron et al., 2023)
on the RAGTruth benchmark (Niu et al., 2024).

4 Methodology
We frame hallucination as an attentionrouting fail-
ure, where attention progressivelyescapesseman-
tically grounded prompt regions toward irrelevant
context or self-reinforcing generation. To charac-
terize this, we propose theAttention Escape Score
(AES), a training-free framework modeling hallu-
cination via three complementary dynamics:Se-
mantic Anchoring,Contextual Distraction, and
Generation Inertia. Formally, given a promptP
and responseR, letC<t = [P;R <t] be the context
at decoding stept, andA(l,h)∈R T×T denote the
attention matrix for layerland headh.

frame, we introduce a span-level semantic align-
ment. We partitionP andR into semantically co-
herent spans{p1,...,p m} and{r1,...,r n} (con-
struction strategies are compared in Appendix G).

features) or late layers (next-token prediction), mid-
dle layers support semantic integration and exhibit
fewer extreme attention sinks (Xiao et al., 2024;
Barbero et al., 2025), providing a stable window

4.4 Detection: Attention Escape Score
These signals capture complementary hallucination
risks: higher SAS lowers risk, while higher UAL
and RAR increase it. We apply a monotonic trans-
form ˜︃SAS = 1/(SAS +ϵ) for numerical stability
and define theAttention Escape Score (AES)as:
AES =λ 1· ˜︃SAS +λ 2·UAL +λ 3·RAR,(11)
whereλ1,λ 2,λ 3 >0 are fixed coefficients. Com-
ponent contributions are analyzed in Section 5.4.
Figure 5 provides a distributional view of sep-
arability.SASachieves the strongest separation,
underscoring semantic anchoring as the key signal.
UALandRARare less discriminative in the bulk
but capture complementary tail cases (diffuse at-
tention and atypical routing). By integrating these
cues,AESenhances separability and stabilizes risk
scores across long contexts. Appendix I further
demonstrates AES’s robustness to moderate coeffi-
cient perturbations, indicating precise weight tun-
ing is unnecessary.
4.5 Mitigation: Pruning Escape Routes
Building on the above analysis, we further pro-
pose PER (Pruning Escape Routes), a lightweight
inference-time intervention method designed to
suppress dominantattention escape routes. PER
adopts a static selection strategy by pre-identifying
a compact, model-specific set of heads that consis-
tently exhibitinterferingbehavior on hallucinated
samples. Specifically, we apply our head-role clas-
sifier on the hallucination split, aggregate per-span
head labels, and select the top-k heads (e.g.,k=10)
with the highest interfering frequency, forming a
fixed intervention setH int.
At inference, for each head (l,h)∈H int, we at-
tenuate its output vector o(l,h)
t by a constant scalar
β∈[0,1) : ˜o(l,h)

the modified head output before the final linear
projection. This static attenuation weakens domi-
nant escape pathways by pruning distraction-prone
channels, effectively reducing hallucinatory drift
with negligible runtime overhead.
5 Experiments
5.1 Experimental Settings
Datasets.We evaluated two public RAG halluci-
nation benchmarks:RAGTruth(Niu et al., 2024),
which provides span-level hallucination annota-
tions across multiple domains and generation tasks;
andDolly (AC)(Hu et al., 2024), an instruction-
following benchmark under the Accurate Context
setting with manually curated hallucination labels
from Sun et al. (2025). Detailed statistics of the
datasets are provided in Appendix A.
Models.We evaluated AES in various LLM fam-
ilies, includingLlama2(7B, 13B),Llama3-8B,
andMistral-7B, to assess whether attention escape
dynamics is generalized across architectures and
scales. Importantly, unless otherwise specified, all
experiments use thesame configuration and ag-
gregation coefficientsacross models and datasets
without per-model or per-dataset retuning.
Baselines.We compare against eight repre-
sentative detectors spanning four paradigms:
uncertainty-basedmethods (Perplexity (Ren
et al., 2023), LN-Entropy (Malinin and Gales,
2021));cross-sample consistencymethods (Self-
CheckGPT (Manakul et al., 2023), Eigen-
Score (Chen et al., 2024));self-evaluationmeth-
ods (P(True) (Kadavath et al., 2022)); andinternal-
signalmethods, including supervised probing over
hidden states (ICR Probe (Zhang et al., 2025)) and
utilization-based diagnostics of contextual ground-
ing and parametric contribution (ReDeEP (Sun
et al., 2025), LUMINA (Yeh et al., 2025)). De-
tails are provided in Appendix B.

5.3 Analysis of Alignment and Attention
Baselines
Beyond Semantic Alignment and Coarse Atten-
tion.Since AES uses semantic alignment to es-
tablish a reference frame for attention analysis, we
further examine whether its performance can be ex-
plained by semantic similarity alone. We compare
AES with two alignment-only baselines: Align-
MaxSim, which uses the maximum similarity be-
tween a generated span and context spans, and
Align-MeanTop3, which averages similarities over
the three most aligned spans. We further include
three simple attention baselines that measure at-
tention allocated to retrieved evidence, top-aligned
spans, or previously generated tokens.
As shown in Table 5, both alignment-only and
attention-only baselines substantially underperform
AES. This indicates that neither semantic matching
nor coarse attention allocation alone is sufficient for
reliable hallucination detection. Instead, AES ben-
efits from jointly modeling complementary routing
behaviors through Semantic Anchoring, Contextual
Distraction, and Generation Inertia.

6 Conclusion
In this work, we study RAG hallucinations from
the perspective of attention routing and show that
failures to utilize relevant retrieved evidence can be
characterized through attention dynamics. We pro-
pose AES, a training-free framework that captures
three complementary aspects of attention escape:
weakenedSemantic Anchoring, increasedContex-
tual Distraction, and heightenedGeneration Iner-
tia. Experiments across multiple datasets and LLM
families show robust detection under a fixed hyper-
parameter configuration, with cross-dataset results
supporting the stability of the identified routing
patterns. We further introduce PER, a lightweight
inference-time intervention that attenuates atten-
tion heads associated with systematic escape routes.
PER effectively reduces hallucination while largely
preserving general knowledge and multi-hop rea-
soning capabilities, suggesting that targeted atten-
tion intervention can provide a practical direction
for improving RAG reliability.
Limitations
Despite the promising results of our framework
for diagnosing and mitigating RAG hallucinations,
several limitations remain.First, our method re-
quires access to internal activations and attention
states, limiting its applicability to closed-source or
API-only models.Second, AES relies on response-
conditioned span-level alignment to define a se-
mantic reference frame for measuring attention
routing. Although this approach is robust across
encoders and span granularities (Appendix G), the
alignment may not match gold supporting evidence,
especially in domains with technical or implicit ev-
idence.Finally, our evaluation focuses on standard
RAG settings with sufficiently relevant and reliable
retrieved evidence. Extending AES/PER to noisy
retrieval and developing causal measurements to
disentangle contextual and parametric effects re-
main important directions for future work.
Ethical Considerations
This work does not involve the collection, process-
ing, or inference of sensitive personal information.
All experiments are conducted on publicly avail-
able benchmarks in compliance with standard re-
search ethics and data usage policies. Our goal is to
improve the reliability and transparency of LLMs
in RAG systems by identifying and mitigating hal-
lucinated outputs. Although such techniques could
be misused to optimize deceptive systems, our con-
tributions are intended to support safer deployment
and evaluation of AI systems. AI-assisted tools
were used exclusively for language refinement and
presentation clarity, they did not contribute to the
design of the proposed methods.

A Details of Datasets
We conduct experiments on two widely used hal-
lucination benchmarks covering both retrieval-
augmented and general generation scenarios.
RAGTruth.RAGTruth is a benchmark specif-
ically designed for hallucination detection in
retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) settings,
containing 15,090 training data and 2,700 testing
data. Each instance consists of a user query, a
set of retrieved documents, and an LLM-generated
response annotated with span-level hallucination
labels. The dataset covers multiple generation
tasks, including question answering, summariza-
tion and data-to-text generation, enabling compre-
hensive evaluation across diverse reasoning sce-
narios. RAGTruth provides responses generated
by six different LLMs: Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B,
Llama2-70B, Mistral-7B, GPT-3.5 and GPT-4. In
our experiments, we adopt the extended test set
created by Sun et al. (2025), which includes anno-
tations for Llama3-8b.
Dolly (AC).The Dolly (AC) benchmark is
derived from thedatabricks-dolly-15k 2, an
instruction-following dataset covering a wide range
of tasks such as summarization, closed-domain
question answering and information extraction.
Following Hu et al. (2024), we adopt theAccurate
Context (AC)setting, where the provided reference
context is assumed to be largely noise-free. Since
hallucination annotations are not natively available
for generated responses in Dolly, we adopt the man-
ually curated hallucination labels provided by Sun
et al. (2025), which contains 300 data. These an-
notations are available for responses generated by
Llama2-7B, Llama2-13B and Llama3-8B, which
are the models evaluated on the Dolly (AC) bench-
mark in our experiments.

D Implementation Details
All experiments are conducted using open-weight
Transformer-based LLMs. Inference is performed
on NVIDIA H200 GPUs. Attention statistics are ex-
tracted at the final decoding step using a streaming
implementation to avoid recomputation overhead.
For the AES in Eq. 11, we fix the coefficients as
λ1 = 0.25,λ2 = 0.35 andλ3 = 0.4 across all ex-
periments. We select these values on thetraining
split (via validation on held-out training data) and
report all results on thetestsplit, keeping the coeffi-
cients unchanged across models and datasets. More
parameter analysis can be found in Appendix I.

For the threshold τ in Eq. 6, we set τ= 0.65
to filter out weak or semantically unrelated span
pairs. This value is chosen empirically to suppress
incidental semantic overlap while preserving mean-
ingful prompt–response alignment. We observe
that AES performance is not highly sensitive to
moderate variations ofτ, and additional analysis is
provided in Appendix I.
We defineLmid ={⌊ρ sL⌋,...,⌊ρ eL⌋} in Sec-
tion 4.3.1 with ρs = 0.25 andρe = 0.875 for all
models(e.g., for 32 layers, including layer 8-28).
We setϵ= 1 in Section 4.4 across all experiments.
Span segmentation.In our implementation,
spans are constructed using sentence-level seg-
mentation based on regular expressions. We also
experiment with alternative segmentation strate-
gies, including syntactic parsing and finer-grained
chunking. While finer-grained spans theoretically
increase the number of alignment computations,
we find that the overall detection overhead remains
low in practice, even with substantially increased
span counts. Detailed comparisons of different
span granularities are reported in Appendix G.
Attention head activation and role thresholds.
Following Section 4.2, we identify functionally
active attention heads based on their response-to-
prompt attention strength. Specifically, we set the
activation threshold to 0.025, and use a top-k selec-
tion strategy with top_k_ratio= 0.05 to account
for the sparsity of attention distributions. A head is
classified asusefulif its Semantic Union Coverage
(SUC) exceeds 0.2, and asinterferingif its SUC
is below 0.05. These thresholds are fixed across
all experiments and models. The gap between the
useful and interfering thresholds is intentionally
introduced to improve robustness to attention vari-
ability and to avoid forcing ambiguous heads into
hard categories. Heads with intermediate SUC val-
ues are treated as neutral and excluded from inter-
vention, as they often serve auxiliary roles (e.g.,
syntactic organization) and are neither consistently
beneficial nor harmful to semantic grounding.
Intervention details.We instantiate the pro-
posedescape-route pruningwith a fixed interven-
tion set and a constant attenuation factor. To con-
struct the intervention setHint, we run our head-
role classifier on thehallucination splitand aggre-
gate head labels across response spans. For each
head (l,h) , we compute itsinterfering frequency,
i.e., the fraction of spans for which the head is clas-
sified asinterfering. We then select thetop-k heads
with the highest interfering frequency and fix them
asH int; unless otherwise stated, we usek=20.
At inference time, we apply a static attenuation
to each intervened head:

I Parameter Sensitivity Analysis
We analyze the robustness of AES with respect
to two types of parameters: (i) aggregation coeffi-
cients that balance different attention signals and
(ii) the semantic alignment threshold that defines
span-level relevance. Unless otherwise stated, all
experiments in this section are conducted using the
Llama2-7B-chatmodel on theRAGTruthdataset,
with all other components kept fixed.
Sensitivity to aggregation coefficients.TheAt-
tention Escape Score (AES)aggregates three com-
plementary attention-based signals via a linear com-
bination:
AES =λ 1· 1
SAS + 1+λ2·UAL+λ 3·RAR.(13)
To assess whether the effectiveness of AES de-
pends on precise coefficient values or reflects intrin-
sic properties of the proposed signals, we conduct
a parameter sensitivity analysis. Rather than tun-
ing for optimal performance, this analysis aims to
evaluate therobustnessof AES under moderate
coefficient perturbations.
Specifically, we vary each coefficient indepen-
dently over a deliberately wider range than the val-
ues observed in our tuned settings, while keeping
the other coefficients fixed. This serves as a stress
test, verifying that AES remains stable even under
substantial weight perturbations.

• Contextual Distraction Weight: λ2 ∈

• Generation Inertia Weight:λ 3∈[0.15,0.4]
This results in a total of 196 coefficient combi-
nations. For each configuration, we evaluate hallu-

Robustness Statistics.As summarized in Ta-
ble 10, AES exhibits strong robustness to coef-
ficient variation. Across all 196 configurations, the
standard deviation of AUROC is only0.0028, and
the total performance range (maximum minus min-
imum) is approximately1.1%. A similarly low
variance is observed for Pearson correlation.
Notably, even under the worst-performing co-

achieves an AUROC of0.8639, which remains
substantially higher than the strongest baseline re-
ported in our experiments. These results indicate
that AES performance is not driven by fine-grained
coefficient tuning, but instead arises from the sta-

attention routing signals.

K Error Analysis
We analyze representative failure cases of AES at
the response level. Our analysis proceeds in two
steps: (i) stratifying performance by task type to
identify systematic weaknesses and (ii) examining
false positives/negatives to summarize recurring
patterns.
Task-type sensitivity.Performance varies across
task types, with Data2txt being the most stable and
QA being the most challenging. Table 14 summa-
rizes this trend. A plausible explanation is that
QA often involves implicit reasoning or sparse evi-
dence usage, which can dilute span-level anchoring
signals at the response level, while Data2txt tends
to align more directly with localized evidence.
Typical errors.False positives often arise when
grounded responses rely onimplicitordiffuseevi-
dence, leading to weaker concentration on explic-
itly aligned spans and mildly elevated HRS. False
negatives typically occur when unsupported con-
tent is brief, weakly expressed, or tightly inter-
woven with factual statements, making attention
escape less pronounced.
Overall, these results suggest that AES is broadly
applicable across task types: it remains effective
beyond Data2txt. For the more challenging QA
task, it still provides a strong and useful signal.
Importantly, the remaining errors are dominated by
borderline cases near the decision threshold rather
than task-specific failure modes, indicating that the
same attention-routing diagnostic transfers across
tasks without requiring task-specific redesign.
